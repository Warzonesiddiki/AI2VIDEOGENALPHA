# Research & Operating Plan — Original Zombie-Survival Channel

**Research snapshot:** 29 October 2026 (Asia/Kolkata). This is a strategic brief, not a promise of virality, reach, eligibility, or revenue.

## 1. What the research could and could not verify

## 2. Strategic takeaways—hypotheses to test, not algorithm guarantees

1. **One-sentence premise:** viewers should understand the place, constraint, and immediate risk in a few seconds.
2. **Story before template:** recurring characters and a recognizable visual language help a series, but the core event, dilemma, emotional turn, and result must materially differ from episode to episode.
3. **Progress that matters:** keep a canon ledger for time, locations, relationships, supplies, injuries, beliefs, clues, and decisions. A day counter is useful only when the day changes the story.
4. **Specific stakes:** each episode needs a physical objective and a human consequence. “The group finds water” is not enough; who pays, who disagrees, what does it cost, and what new choice follows?
5. **Short-form as a separate edit:** create a short with its own setup, context, pacing, captions, framing, and payoff/cliffhanger. Avoid posting ten near-identical cuts with only the opening line changed.
6. **Distinct visual/sound identity:** design original character silhouettes, palette, line treatment, music/sound motifs, thumbnails, and title voice. Keep a model/style guide and reuse approved assets only where it serves continuity.
7. **Trust beats shock:** fictional survival details should be separated from real-world claims. Source-check any factual statement, avoid dangerous instructions, and label fiction clearly in descriptions/channel information.
8. **No virality promise:** public examples show correlation and surface format, not causal proof. Iterate from your own impressions, click-through, retention, returning viewers, comments, production time, and cost.

## 3. YouTube policy and platform requirements to build around

### Originality / monetization

YouTube’s monetization policy currently says monetized content should be original and authentic, and not mass-produced, generic, repetitive, or manipulative. It specifically says a series following recurring characters may be eligible when each video has a distinct storyline, focus, or concept; highly similar storylines with minimal variation and AI templates that look mass-produced are risky. This does **not** mean that using AI is automatically disallowed, nor that any checklist guarantees YPP approval. The channel is reviewed as a whole.

Official policy: <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en>.

### Synthetic/altered-media disclosure

YouTube requires disclosure when altered or synthetic content appears realistic in a way that could mislead viewers. Its guidance says non-realistic animation and production assistance such as outlines, scripts, titles, thumbnails, or captions generally do not require the same disclosure; realistic scenes, altered real events/places, or certain realistic synthetic media do. The app must present a **human-reviewed disclosure checklist** for every upload and should not silently decide “AI used = disclose” or “fiction = no disclosure.” Use the current YouTube Studio question and policy at upload time. Disclosure alone is not stated to reduce reach or monetization eligibility.

Official policy: <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en>.

### Shorts and long-form

YouTube Help currently describes square/vertical videos up to three minutes as Shorts for eligible standard-channel uploads. It also states that Shorts over one minute with an active copyright claim can be blocked globally. Use a 9:16 master for Shorts, a separate 16:9 master for long-form, and original or properly licensed audio. Verify current classification and music rules at upload time.

Official Shorts guidance: <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en>.

For monetized long-form videos of at least eight minutes, creators can enable mid-rolls; YouTube notes that natural breakpoints are preferable to interruptions mid-sentence or mid-action, and ad slots are not guaranteed to serve. Treat ad placement as a viewer-experience decision, not a reason to pad scripts.

Official mid-roll guidance: <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6175006?hl=en>.

### Monetization milestones and 2027 notice

The current expanded-YPP help page lists earlier access in eligible regions at 500 subscribers, 3 valid public uploads in the last 90 days, and either 3,000 qualified watch hours in the last 12 months or 3 million qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days. Full ad/Premium revenue thresholds are listed there as 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 qualified public watch hours in the last 12 months or 10 million qualified public Shorts views in the last 90 days. India appears in the eligible-region list on that page. The official YPP changes page also currently says that **starting 1 February 2027**, new YPP ad/Premium entry thresholds are scheduled to change to 8,000 qualified watch hours or 20 million qualified Shorts views, alongside 1,000 subscribers. Because policies and eligibility can change, use the channel’s Earn tab and the official help pages as the source of truth when applying; do not build a business plan around a threshold snapshot.

Official pages:
- <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13429240?hl=en>
- <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12843009?hl=en>

### YouTube API publishing

The Data API supports video uploads with OAuth. There is an important official-documentation conflict to handle rather than guess: the current `videos.insert` reference says uploads from unverified API projects are **not** restricted to private viewing, while the API revision history and YouTube Help still describe private locks for uploads from unverified API services. Treat the actual project/channel behavior as unresolved until tested: explicitly send `status.privacyStatus=private` (the current method reference says omission can default to public), read back the returned privacy status, confirm the result in Studio, and use manual Studio handoff if public API publishing has not been proven for the selected project. Do not state that an audit is always required—or that it is never required—without checking current official guidance and the project's status. Separate Google Cloud API quota from channel upload limits; these can differ. Do not scrape YouTube Studio or bypass platform constraints. Require human approval of visibility, schedule, audience setting, metadata, rights, and AI disclosure before public release.

As of this research snapshot, the current `videos.insert` method reference states 100 calls/day and 1 unit per call in the separate Video Uploads quota bucket. This is not permission for a channel to upload 100 videos per day: channel limits are separate, and quota allocations/project settings may change. Re-check the method reference and the selected Google Cloud project's quota/limit screens at implementation and before scaling. The 2026 granular-quota transition also makes old quota tables especially unsafe to copy without checking the project's current allocation.

Official references that currently need reconciliation: <https://developers.google.com/youtube/data/reference/rest/v3/videos/insert>, <https://developers.google.com/youtube/data/revision_history>, and <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7300965?hl=en>. See also YouTube's Studio upload guide and feature-access/daily-limit guidance: <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/57407?hl=en> and <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9890437?hl=en>.

## 4. The “10 videos per day” goal—capacity is not the same as cadence

Ten **15-minute** uploads a day would publish 150 minutes (2.5 hours) of long-form content every day, or 912.5 hours per year if sustained. That is not a credible first-stage quality target for a new original animation channel. It invites rushed stories, repeated scenes, weak review, escalating generation cost, rights mistakes, and a channel catalogue that looks mass-produced. The policy risk is not “AI used”; the risk is material that feels interchangeable or exists primarily to fill a quota.

Recommended launch cadence:

| Stage | Long-form | Shorts | Gate to progress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pilot, weeks 1–4 | 1 every 7–10 days | 2–4 distinct shorts per week | Human approves every asset; confirm production cost and audience response |
| Validation, weeks 5–12 | 1–2 per week | 4–7 per week | Improve a measured weakness; avoid superficial title/thumbnail changes as the only variation |
| Scaled, after 90 days | Increase only if quality, retention, rights, review, and unit economics hold | Daily only if each short is a worthwhile edit | A human editorial sign-off, cost ceiling, fact/rights audit, and no backlog of unresolved continuity |

The application may **prepare** more candidate assets in batches, but its scheduler should have conservative configurable caps. Keep unsubmitted content in local draft state; if a user approves an API upload, upload as private by default. Build a “10 slots/day” calendar as an optional planning view, not as a mandate to publish ten pieces. The operator may raise limits after documented review. No engagement buying, fake views, repetitive spam, misleading metadata, or automated comment/engagement manipulation.

## 5. Original series format for this project

The attached series seed, **The Quiet Hours: Ternfall**, is an original urban survival saga. It is not a Beanie Survives clone: the lead is a water-network engineer in a fictional coastal delta city; the story uses an ensemble and a distinct visual language; the main tension concerns brittle emergency infrastructure, community trust, and decentralized warning systems. Infected behavior and outbreak rules are fictional. The show’s survival facts, if any, must be researched separately and tagged in the script as sourced, uncertain, or entirely fictional.

For each 15-minute episode, plan roughly **2,100–2,400 spoken words** at 140–160 wpm, then measure actual narration duration. Each episode must have a distinctive dramatic design—not a fixed “day-counter, resource-card, scare, cliffhanger” machine. Mix quieter relationship chapters, procedural problem-solving, discovery, negotiation, evacuation, moral dilemmas, aftermath, and action. Let scripts land where the story supports them; do not add filler merely to hit a runtime.

## 6. Production system and minimum viable team

### Roles (one person may initially cover several)

- **Showrunner/story editor:** approves canon, episode premise, and revisions.
- **Research/fact checker:** verifies real-world claims; records sources and access dates.
- **Script editor:** checks character voice, pacing, logic, originality, and story variation.
- **Art director:** owns original visual bible, character sheets, composition and asset continuity.
- **Producer/editor:** controls generation jobs, selection, sound design, timeline, export, and QC.
- **Rights/publishing operator:** checks commercial rights, release forms/voices/music, metadata, disclosures, privacy, and final upload approval.

Do not let the same unattended agent approve its own script, facts, image rights, finished render, and public upload.

### Pipeline states

`Idea → Research → Pitch Approved → Outline → Continuity Review → Script Draft → Script Edit → Facts/Rights Review → Scene Plan → Visual/Audio Generation → Edit/Assembly → Technical QC → Human Editorial QC → Metadata/Disclosure Review → Local Draft/Preflight → Human Upload Approval → Private Upload (optional) or Manual Studio Handoff → Human Public/Schedule Approval → Published/Scheduled → Analytics Review`.

Every transition records user, timestamp, version, prior artifact hash, provider/model, estimated/actual cost, sources, rights evidence, and review outcome. A rejected item retains its revision history; nothing silently overwrites approved canon or a rendered file.

### End-to-end autoproduction operating profile

The app should make the common path one clear **Produce Episode** action, not a maze of disconnected prompts. The operator selects the series/episode or supplies a premise, chooses long-form and/or Shorts, confirms provider preset, output location, production timezone if scheduling is requested, and pre-authorizes a finite spend cap. Once the series bible and setup are approved, the workflow can automatically draft the premise/outline/script, check continuity and real-world claims, plan scenes, call configured generators, create narration/captions, render, run technical QC, and assemble metadata and an upload-ready folder. This satisfies the user's goal of AI doing most routine production without pretending the integrations are already built.

Use durable, resumable stage jobs and preserve partial outputs. Stop only at configured editorial review gates, missing/uncertain rights or source evidence, safety/continuity conflicts, failed QC, provider errors, unknown charges, or a budget cap. Offer stepwise review for the pilot and supervised autopilot for routine stages after approval. The final package always gets a human review; public visibility, scheduling, paid promotion, audience selection, AI disclosure, and rights determinations are never silently delegated to the model. The app then provides a per-file, step-by-step Studio checklist with the actual filenames and metadata, plus the manual workflow in `12_YOUTUBE_UPLOAD_RUNBOOK.md`.

This is an **automation target**, not a guarantee of flawless generations, platform acceptance, monetization, views, or revenue. Show uncertainty, measured QC evidence, costs, and actionable repair paths.

### Per-episode deliverables

- Series/season/episode identifiers, title options, logline, content notes, and unique premise.
- Episode outline with A/B plot, character decisions, causal consequences, cliffhanger/payoff, and continuity deltas.
- Full narration script with word count, estimated/actual TTS duration, scene anchors, speaker labels, pronunciation guide, and caption-safe text.
- Research ledger: each real-world factual claim, citation, access date, verification status, and source-quality note. Mark unverified claims and exclude them from narration until reviewed.
- Scene manifest, character/location reference IDs, visual prompts, provider/model, seed/reference assets where supported, and rights evidence.
- Voice and music licenses/consents, narration tracks, sound-effect provenance, mix notes.
- 16:9 long-form MP4, 9:16 Shorts variants, SRT/VTT captions, thumbnail, chapter timings, title/description/tag suggestions, AI-disclosure decision record, and QC report.
- A cost report and reproducibility manifest linking input versions to generated assets and render settings.

## 7. Cost and income plan

### Cost controls

Do not market the production stack as free. Separate:

1. script/model tokens;
2. still image generations and retries;
3. generated video seconds (usually the expensive/high-variance component);
4. TTS/voice usage;
5. storage, backup, and bandwidth;
6. editing/render compute and electricity;
7. music/stock licenses and any human review/editing.

Track estimated and actual spend per scene, episode, format, and provider. Let a user set per-episode and monthly caps; pause before exceeding a cap; support lower-cost 2D/parallax motion and manual assets as a clearly disclosed fallback. Keep retry ceilings, cancellation, and idempotency so a timeout does not spend twice unnoticed.

### Revenue ladder (not guaranteed)

1. Build repeat viewing and playlists; apply for YPP only when eligible and compliant.
2. Test memberships/fan support with honest behind-the-scenes, art/process notes, and story discussions.
3. Develop original merch only after the title, logo, characters, and symbols are cleared and audience demand exists.
4. Explore sponsorships or affiliate offers only when genuinely relevant and transparently disclosed.
5. License or adapt original stories into comics/ebooks/compilations only after chain-of-title and commercial-use rights are documented.
6. Keep a simple monthly dashboard: production cost, long-form/Shorts view metrics, watch time/retention, returning viewers, subscriber conversion, revenue by source, and margin per published episode.

Never promise RPM, subscriber growth, a particular view count, “passive income,” or a guaranteed monetization date. Revisit platform requirements and provider terms before each major scale-up.

## 8. Google AI Studio + Windows/OpenCode architecture decision

Google AI Studio Build is a **web-app builder**. Its current documentation describes a React front end and Node.js server runtime, server-side secrets, GitHub sync, ZIP export, and Cloud Run deployment. Exported code requires environment configuration outside AI Studio. A hosted Cloud Run app does not gain access to the Windows PC’s folders, local FFmpeg binary, or local OpenCode process just because the UI was built in AI Studio.

Recommended workflow:

1. Build the full-stack web app in AI Studio.
2. Sync to GitHub or export ZIP.
3. Run the code locally on the PC with a local API/worker that owns project storage and invokes FFmpeg.
4. Run OpenCode from the repository as a separate CLI coding/production agent. Give it a documented, narrow CLI and structured artifact files—not unrestricted credentials or a public-publishing button.
5. Use a server-side/provider adapter for Gemini and other media APIs; never ship API keys to browser code. Store secrets locally using a safe mechanism and keep `.env*`, OAuth tokens, and generated private assets out of Git.
6. If OpenCode is used on Windows, check its current official Windows guidance; WSL is recommended for the best compatibility. If serving any local UI/server, bind to loopback for local-only access, secure it, and do not expose OpenCode or provider credentials on the LAN. Keep public Cloud Run deployment disabled by default; any later hosted/multi-user mode needs a separate authenticated, isolated, quota-controlled security/privacy design.

Official AI Studio Build guide: <https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/aistudio-build-mode>.
Official OpenCode Windows guidance: <https://opencode.ai/docs/windows-wsl/>.
Official OpenCode permissions: <https://opencode.ai/docs/permissions/>.

## 9. 90-day execution plan

### Days 1–14 — research and product foundation

- Verify brand/domain/handle availability and do preliminary title/logo/character trademark checks.
- Approve the original show bible, first 10 episode premises, visual style guide, content boundaries, rights policy, and fact-check format.
- Build a local-first app foundation with durable projects, backups, provider settings, a CLI, and job logs.
- Produce one pilot script and a storyboard manually/with selected providers; estimate real cost per minute and identify the visual weak points.

### Days 15–30 — controlled pilot

- Finish and review one long episode; produce two to four independently edited vertical stories from it.
- Upload privately/unlisted for internal review first; use a human to make all Studio disclosure, audience, monetization, and publish decisions.
- Track script-to-render time, failed generations, actual spend, retention, comments, and viewer questions.
- Do not expand the roadmap beyond the first 20 episode cards until the pilot proves the story voice and visual continuity.

### Days 31–60 — repeatability without sameness

- Publish one or two long videos weekly only if the edit/review capacity is real.
- Build a curated season map; include new setting, conflict type, point of view, emotional theme, and visual motif across episodes.
- Release Shorts as adaptations with independent hooks/context/payoffs, not mechanically duplicated crops.
- Review weak points every 7 days; update prompt versions and keep the change log.

### Days 61–90 — economics and scale decision

- Calculate true cost and labor per finished long-form episode and per short.
- Identify which premises and moments hold attention and which titles/thumbs earn qualified clicks without misleading.
- Add a second editor/reviewer or reduce cadence if QC, continuity, or rights review is being skipped.
- Increase cadence only if the unit economics, viewer response, and originality audits justify it. Ten public uploads/day remains an experiment requiring evidence, not the definition of success.

### Media API facts that affect the app's actual architecture

- **Short video is not a 15-minute episode.** Current Gemini API model docs list stable Gemini Omni Flash as `gemini-omni-1.1-flash`, with one output video of 3–10 seconds at 24 fps and supported 360p/720p/1080p/4K settings. The documented generation/editing path is the Gemini Interactions API. The Gemini API pricing page checked 29 Oct lists no free tier for Omni; standard 720p video output is approximately **US$0.10 per generated second**. The Gemini API's Veo 3.1 Preview IDs (`veo-3.1-generate-preview`, `veo-3.1-fast-generate-preview`, and `veo-3.1-lite-generate-preview`) were shut down on 22 Oct 2026. Do not start a new Gemini API integration using those retired preview IDs. A separate Google Cloud/Agent Platform route must be verified with that product's own current docs and account access; do not assume the preview API IDs work there.
- **Cost implication:** a purely generated 15-minute (900-second) 720p master would require at least 90 maximum-length 10-second clips and have a nominal video-output charge of about **US$90 per episode**, before retries, higher-resolution pricing, images, TTS, storage, rendering or taxes. Ten such episodes/day would be about **US$900/day** for the 720p generated seconds alone. This is a planning calculation from the cited rate, not a quote, quota or guaranteed output. The production wizard must estimate the selected plan from actual shot durations and retry allowance and stop at the user's authorized cap.
- **Illustration and motion are a distinct production path.** Current Gemini image-generation docs use Nano Banana 2.1 (`gemini-nano-banana-2.1`) and describe image generation/editing via the Interactions API. The pricing page lists indicative output costs of about US$0.0336 per 1K image, US$0.0504 per 2K image, and US$0.113 per 4K image; the user must verify current prices/tier. Approved stills plus genuine FFmpeg camera/parallax/transition work can create a real illustrated motion-comic, but must be labelled accurately—not passed off as fully generated video. An operator may instead choose more generated footage after seeing and authorizing the cost.
- **Voice is a separate integration and cost surface.** Google Cloud Text-to-Speech lists Chirp 3: HD at up to 1 million characters free per month, then US$30 per million characters. Its Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS Preview pricing is separate, has no free usage limit, is preview-stage, and is scheduled to change on 1 Jan 2027. These are dated references, not a fixed voice choice. The application must confirm voice/language availability, account/region, current terms and price; voice cloning requires documented speaker rights/consent. The creator must audition and approve the actual narration voice.
- **Safe pilot profile:** generate original approved character/location references and illustrated scene assets, assemble a coherent motion-comic with licensed/approved narration, captions, sound and actual render/QC, then add a small number of short Omni video highlights only if the operator chooses them and the estimate fits the budget. This is a recommended cost-aware starting preset, not a hidden quality downgrade or a limit on later user-approved profiles. Never make an unannounced switch from video to still images when a video model is unavailable.

### Implementation rules derived from this snapshot

1. Treat AI Studio Build's coding agent, OpenCode's optional coding model, and every app runtime provider as separate systems with separate keys, data disclosures, costs and configuration.
2. Don't ask AI Studio to select Opus, assume it can, or use leaderboard rank as a code-quality claim. Use the execution contract in file `14`, perform the build in the ordered phases, and judge success from real tests and a local pilot.
3. In the app, show the exact provider/model/API route selected for **each modality** (text/research, image, video, TTS, transcription, music) and the verified capability/limit/price/date. Keep IDs and limits configurable; block a retired/unknown model rather than falling back silently.
4. For any generated footage, calculate duration-based cost and retries before a paid batch; for an episode, show the total estimate and uncertainty. Keep every media call user-authorized, resumable, cancelable and capped.
5. Use current official provider documentation and legal terms for runtime access. The rankings and prices above are snapshots, not permanent defaults or commitments.
