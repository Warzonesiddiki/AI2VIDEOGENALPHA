# Original Series Bible — *The Quiet Hours: Ternfall*

**Version:** 1.0 story seed, 29 October 2026  
**Format:** serialized illustrated survival thriller; 10 seasons × 10 arcs × 10 episodes = 1,000 planned episodes. Each arc is a ten-episode story block, not a fully drafted ten-episode script.  
**Target runtime:** approximately 15 minutes per episode, adjusted to the finished narration and story.  
**Audience promise:** tense, humane, original survival fiction in which practical systems and relationships matter as much as escape.

> This is fiction, not emergency, medical, public-health, engineering, or survival advice. Any real-world fact mentioned in an episode must be separately researched, cited, and reviewed. The infection and its behavior are invented.

## 1. Series premise

When the emergency sirens fall silent in **Ternfall**, a fictional coastal delta city, the city’s infected begin moving through the drainage districts as though an unheard rhythm still calls them. **Rhea Mistry**, a municipal water-network engineer, seals the South Gate control works with a few frightened survivors. Her brother **Kabir**, a field sound archivist working a community radio route inland, disappears into the dead air.

Rhea discovers that Ternfall’s emergency infrastructure—old flood maps, centralized sirens, pump schedules, sealed records, and one brittle control network—has made survival harder. The sirens did not cause the outbreak, and water is not the magical source of infection. The signal system simply creates repeated patterns that the infected react to in this fictional world. As survivors cross canals, islands, rail cuts, wards, reservoirs, and blacked-out districts, they must decide whether to rebuild one powerful control center or a network of local warnings that people can question and change.

The saga’s main mystery is not “who secretly made the zombies?” It is how people can build a truthful, resilient community when every old institution is incomplete, frightened, or wrong. The ending is about a safer way to live—not a miracle cure that erases every loss.

### One-line pitch

A water engineer maps a city that is learning to move in silence, then has to choose whether the people she saves will depend on one voice—or on each other.

### Series engine

Every episode combines a **specific local objective**, a **human disagreement**, a **change to the group or world**, and a **consequence that survives the episode**. The objective may be a route, a signal, a shelter, a missing person, an agreement, a record, an evacuation, a repair, a memorial, or a decision—not always a fight or a supply run. Some episodes are quiet, procedural, reflective, or relationship-led. Avoid a repeated “find resource / scare / cliffhanger” formula.

## 2. Originality and visual identity

This show is inspired only by broad survival-thriller craft: emotional character arcs, atmosphere, distinct locations, morally difficult choices, mystery, and serialized consequences. It does **not** borrow a character, world rule, scene, panel, dialogue, title, plot sequence, logo, thumbnail layout, or art style from Beanie Survives or any manhwa.

### Visual bible seed

- **Medium:** original hand-inked 2D editorial illustration with layered, slightly imperfect linework, watercolor/paper grain, restrained motion and expressive negative space. Avoid photorealism and avoid copying a recognizable artist or branded animation look.
- **Palette:** deep estuary teal, slate, faded cream paper, rust-red, sodium-amber lights, and occasional signal-coral. Do not make a yellow beanie the series symbol.
- **Silhouette:** characters have distinct human proportions, clothing and posture; no stick-figure mascot. Rhea’s recognizable shape is a cropped rain jacket, rolled map case, and brass pressure gauge—not a hat.
- **Visual motifs:** pressure needles, tide marks, hand-drawn route lines, unlit siren housings, listening faces, water reflections, paper maps that get annotated or corrected.
- **Camera language:** vary wide environmental compositions, hand-held-feeling close work, map/tabletop inserts, long quiet holds, group blocking, and graphic transitions. Do not use the same framing for every scene.
- **Sound identity:** original sparse percussion and environmental sound. Repeating tones are a fictional story motif and must never be used to imply a real warning signal. All music/SFX require documented rights.
- **Titles/thumbnails:** build a unique typography, logo, and composition system after availability and rights checks. Titles should describe the actual episode, not imitate a competitor’s exact title syntax.

## 3. World and fictional rules

### Ternfall

Ternfall is a fictional coastal city of three canal basins, a reclaimed floodplain, old low-pressure water mains, elevated rail, a harbor island chain, an inland service corridor, and a reservoir beyond the ring road. It is not a coded map of Mumbai or any other real city. Place names and engineering details must remain fictional unless a cited real fact is intentionally introduced and checked.

The city was built in layers. Public maps disagree. Some paper records predate the flood barriers; newer electronic maps hide decommissioned accessways. No single character has a complete or infallible view of the system.

### The Still (fictional infected)

- “The Still” is a name survivors give the infected; it is not a real disease or medical category.
- Transmission in this invented world occurs through a bite or infected saliva entering broken skin. It is not airborne and is not caused by drinking city water. Characters do not know this with certainty at the beginning; the narrative reveals it through evidence and expert caution over multiple arcs.
- The Still are not supernatural, psychic, or secretly strategic. They retain fragments of motor habit—turning toward a familiar vibration, reaching for a door plate, repeating the last few steps of a route—but not coherent memories, speech, or hidden plans.
- They can respond to repeated low mechanical rhythms, especially when the city’s old alert/pump network produces a stable pulse. This is an entirely fictional behavior, not real biology and not advice about how to avoid danger.
- The pulse does not create or control the infection. It can redirect movement in an unreliable way; using it can endanger other people. The story treats the signal as an ethical choice, not a superpower or a simple “zombie hack.”
- They move at a physically plausible pace for the scene; they are not arbitrarily fast/slow to force a cliffhanger. Their behavior is consistent, but individual scenes remain uncertain.
- Graphic gore is not a selling point. Emphasize suspense, sound, distance, fear, hesitation, and consequences. No gratuitous injuries.

### Facts and fiction boundary

All infection rules above are invented. Any practical claim about water storage, electricity, fire, medicine, evacuation, communications, flood behavior, or infrastructure must be cited in a research ledger and reviewed. If a claim cannot be verified, rewrite it as a character’s uncertain belief or omit it. Do not provide dangerous step-by-step survival instructions.

## 4. Main cast

### Rhea Mistry — 31, municipal water-network engineer

Observant, precise, and skilled at reading pressure changes, old maps, and failing systems. Rhea’s weakness is believing that the right measurement can make a choice morally clean. She signed a technical memo approving a route closure before the outbreak; the decision was reasonable on incomplete data but later prevents access to a shelter. Her arc is not “become a fearless leader”; it is learning to show uncertainty, share tools, admit responsibility, and let the people affected help decide.

**Visual anchor:** cropped slate-teal rain jacket with two signal-coral cuff tabs, rolled map case, brass pressure gauge. Hair and facial details should be locked in the approved character sheet. No yellow hat or borrowed mascot.

### Kabir Mistry — 26, field sound archivist and community-radio volunteer

Rhea’s younger brother. He records ordinary city sounds and preserves local voices, not because he is a genius hacker but because he believes lost places should be remembered. He and Rhea have avoided a hard family conversation for years. His voice appears intermittently through recordings and relay fragments before they meet again. He is capable of mistakes and must not become a convenient exposition device.

### Mara Ilyan — 47, ferry mechanic

A talented practical problem-solver who knows the harbor’s working routes and which boats can still move. She distrusts institutions because people she depended on were left waiting during an earlier storm response. Her arc tests the difference between skepticism and refusing any agreement.

### Dr. Amala Nwosu — 38, community epidemiologist

Cautious, compassionate, and exact about what evidence can and cannot establish. She rejects panic and false certainty. She is not a miracle-cure machine or an on-screen source of universal medical advice. Her long arc is about research ethics, consent, community trust, and how to communicate uncertainty.

### Yusef “Yuse” Rahal — 17, rooftop courier and amateur radio learner

A sharp, restless courier who can read roofs and service ladders better than official routes. He wants to be treated as useful without being turned into a child soldier. His choices have consequences; older characters must protect his agency and safety rather than use him as disposable peril.

### Tomas Vale — 53, former emergency director

A competent administrator whose preference for central command comes from real experience of chaotic evacuations. He has withheld some information because he believed panic would kill people. He is an antagonist when his control blocks consent, but never a cartoon villain; he must face consequences, change policy through action, and earn no automatic forgiveness.

### Sima Dey — 68, municipal archive librarian

A keeper of paper flood maps, public hearing minutes, neighborhood names, and corrections nobody entered into the system. She is funny, exacting, and not merely a walking archive. Her survival skill is knowing which story was written out of the record and asking who benefits from the omission.

### Linh Cho — 42, elevated-rail operator

A patient transit operator with a strong sense of route discipline and a complicated loyalty to colleagues left behind. Linh becomes a bridge between formal schedules and human improvisation, then learns that a route is not a promise unless people can opt out.

## 5. Factions and tensions

- **South Gate Survivors:** the first group around Rhea; practical and wary, not a unified army.
- **The Canal Commons:** a loose collection of neighborhoods coordinating water access, routes, care and information. Its members disagree and can change their minds.
- **Continuity Directorate:** the remnants of municipal emergency operations, initially organized around Tomas. Some people there save lives; some use urgency to hoard authority.
- **Harbor Relay:** boat crews, island residents and radio volunteers who exchange messages, batteries, maps and favors under uncertain trust.
- **Upstream Cooperative:** farm, repair and settlement networks beyond the city ring; each has its own rules, needs and political disagreements.
- **The Basin Council:** a rotating governance experiment, not a perfect solution. Its difficult questions include representation, consent, safety, ration fairness, who controls alerts and who is allowed to leave.

No faction is “all good” or “all evil.” Show material interests, history, internal disagreement, and consequences.

## 6. Season-level trajectory

The season boundary is a production/marketing unit, not a promise that the story resets every 100 episodes. The planned story spans roughly 18–24 months, with deliberate time jumps. Each episode’s exact calendar date and location should be stored in the app’s canon ledger. Ten to twenty episodes may occur within a short emergency window; later arcs can cover weeks or months. Do not force one episode to equal one day.

### Season 1 — The First City (E001–E100)
Rhea’s shift crew survives the initial siren failure, gathers a cross-section of residents, discovers that the city’s old map hides a route, and learns that reopening infrastructure may draw the infected toward a shelter. The first community forms through a difficult public choice.

### Season 2 — The Canal Commons (E101–E200)
The group tries to build a fair neighborhood network across canals. Water rumors, barter, signal misuse, storms, and the question of who gets to decide expose the limits of heroic problem-solving.

### Season 3 — The Island Relay (E201–E300)
A harbor crossing becomes an experiment in communication, mutual aid, and trust. Rhea follows fragments of Kabir’s archive but learns that hearing someone’s voice does not mean knowing their present situation.

### Season 4 — The Inland Cut (E301–E400)
The survivors cross an inland route, meet cooperatives and camps with their own histories, and confront the cost of carrying a centralized plan into communities that did not consent to it.

### Season 5 — The Dry Ward (E401–E500)
A care network forces the group to distinguish evidence from rumor, safety from control, and quarantine from punishment. Amala’s findings narrow the fictional infection rules without producing a cure.

### Season 6 — The Long Current (E501–E600)
The team travels upstream through repair stations, reservoir settlements and broken transport routes. Their goal shifts from finding a person to keeping an information chain alive across distance.

### Season 7 — The Basin Council (E601–E700)
The region attempts shared governance. The hard conflict is not “who is secretly evil?” but whether a common alert system can be trusted, contested, repaired, and refused.

### Season 8 — The Long Blackout (E701–E800)
The network fails. Characters must work with partial maps, darkness, paper records, agreed signals and human messengers. The blackout reveals which systems were genuinely shared and which only looked shared.

### Season 9 — The Return Current (E801–E900)
Rhea’s group returns toward Ternfall through settlements changed by the same crisis. The team must decide what can be reopened, who has a right to return, and how to tell the truth about old mistakes.

### Season 10 — Open Frequency (E901–E1000)
The old city is not restored to its previous state. The survivors dismantle a single brittle warning dependency and build a locally governed network of routes, records and signal choices. Rhea and Kabir reconcile through action, not a speech that erases their past. The Still remain a danger; the ending is a durable, imperfect community and a new map with room for corrections.

## 8. Pilot episode beat sheet — E001, “The Eight-Beat Failure”

This is an outline for the app to turn into a full, original script. It is not final narration and does not contain verified real-world survival claims.

- **Cold open (0:00–0:25):** A brass pressure gauge twitches eight times. The official alarm screen stays blank. Below South Gate, a service corridor answers with a rhythm nobody programmed.
- **Premise (0:25–1:30):** Rhea is finishing an overnight pressure survey while the city’s emergency system reports no incident. A text from Kabir asks whether she can still hear the pump channel; she dismisses it as an old family argument.
- **Inciting failure (1:30–3:00):** A flood barrier’s status becomes contradictory: the digital panel says open, the analog dial says closed, and the lower corridor loses its work lights.
- **Human encounter (3:00–5:00):** Rhea finds Mara and two night-shift workers at a locked service hatch. One insists a neighbor is still inside; another wants the gate shut before the water rises.
- **First evidence (5:00–7:00):** The group sees several Still turning toward the repeated mechanical rhythm, not toward a voice calling from the hatch. Rhea does not know why. She stops the pump pulse for a few seconds; the movement changes, but not predictably.
- **Moral choice (7:00–9:30):** The hatch cannot be opened without shifting the gate. Rhea explains the risk and asks the trapped residents what they choose; one wants to wait for a second route, while another asks the group to leave.
- **Action and consequence (9:30–12:30):** The crew makes a controlled, time-limited gate change to open a higher passage. They get the workers out but lose access to the clean service corridor and the panel’s last reliable record.
- **Aftermath (12:30–14:20):** Rhea writes a paper map from memory and marks it uncertain. A radio fragment carries Kabir’s voice, cut off after he names an inland relay.
- **Cliffhanger (14:20–15:00):** The citywide alarm is still silent. At the far end of the dark canal, the same eight-beat pulse begins from a second location.

### Pilot requirements for script generation

- Target approximately 2,175 spoken words at 145 WPM, then adjust to the chosen voice and actual timing.
- Use close third-person centered on Rhea; include concise dialogue from the ensemble. Do not imitate another creator’s narration cadence, wording, scene structure, or voice.
- No real-world emergency instruction, medical diagnosis, or unsupported technical claim. Clearly present the infection and signal behavior as fiction. If an ordinary engineering fact is included, cite it in the research ledger and require human verification.
- Non-graphic, suspense-led. The danger comes from uncertainty and the consequences of choices, not gore.
- End with a consequence and an unresolved story question; do not use a generic “follow for part two” inside the story itself.

## 10. Roadmap expansion protocol for OpenCode / the app

1. Import this file and parse the bible, characters, rules, and 100 arc destinations. Show any parse warnings.
2. Expand **one arc at a time** into ten distinct episode cards. The user can choose a smaller batch size; never exceed 20 cards per model call.
3. For each card, propose a unique physical objective, interpersonal decision, setting and consequence. Vary POV, tone, conflict type, and visual design. Avoid consecutive episodes with the same core problem and outcome.
4. Run continuity checks against all approved canon and neighboring arcs. Unresolved questions remain unresolved until the showrunner approves a reveal.
5. Run originality/similarity warnings against the project’s prior cards and the explicit no-copy guardrails. Never use protected reference material as image input.
6. Ask the showrunner to revise/approve the episode cards. Only approved cards can be expanded into full scripts.
7. Generate one complete 15-minute draft at a time, save as a new version, and separately review script quality, facts, rights, policy, and continuity.
8. Do not generate episodes 1001+ until the first 1,000-episode backbone has been reviewed and the showrunner approves a continuation arc. Possible later stories should grow from characters and consequences, not endless extension of the same premise.
